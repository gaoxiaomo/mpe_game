\begin{abstract}
This paper presents a cooperative control framework for multi-pursuer multi-evader pursuit--evasion (PE) games under nonlinear dynamics with actuator saturation. The framework integrates three layers: (i)~efficient value-function approximation via the V-SNAC architecture, which derives saturated Nash equilibrium control policies directly from the value-function gradient and reduces the number of neural networks from $2(N_p{+}N_e)$ to $N_p$ compared to conventional actor-critic structures; (ii)~dynamic target assignment through a graph-theoretic pairwise swap algorithm that improves team cohesion in real time; and (iii)~communication-based coordination among pursuers targeting the same evader, realized through an augmented value function that combines the learned tracking value with an analytical coordination potential. The augmented value function decomposes into a learned component capturing individual pursuit optimality and an analytical repulsion potential encoding spatial diversity, with the pursuer control derived from the combined gradient inside a $\tanh$ saturation. We prove that this decomposition satisfies an augmented Hamilton--Jacobi--Isaacs equation and connects to potential-based reward shaping. Experiments on a six-degree-of-freedom nonlinear aircraft model across five team configurations (3-vs-1 through 8-vs-4) demonstrate that the coordination potential consistently increases inter-pursuer spatial separation while maintaining capture performance, with graceful degradation under 15\% per-edge communication dropout.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

Multi-agent pursuit--evasion (PE) games constitute a fundamental problem at the intersection of adversarial decision-making and cooperative control. Applications range from UAV swarm interception \cite{b_zhang2023} and satellite engagement \cite{b_gong2020} to perimeter defense \cite{b_shishika2020} and urban airspace security \cite{b_collision_uav}. When the pursuer team outnumbers the evaders, the central challenge is to exploit this numerical advantage through principled cooperation \cite{b_isaacs,b_chen2016,b_yan2022}.

The theoretical foundation for two-player PE games rests on the Hamilton--Jacobi--Isaacs (HJI) equation \cite{b_isaacs}, whose solution yields saddle-point equilibrium strategies. However, extending HJI-based analysis to multi-agent nonlinear systems faces the curse of dimensionality. Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a data-driven route to approximate HJI solutions without requiring full model knowledge \cite{b_lewis1998,b_lopez2020,b_xu2024}. In the multi-agent RL domain, the MADDPG framework \cite{b_maddpg} introduced centralized-training decentralized-execution for mixed cooperative-competitive settings, while communication-aware architectures \cite{b_commrl2025} improve cooperation through explicit message passing. On the differential game side, graphical game formulations model strategic interactions over communication topologies \cite{b_lopez2020,b_graphgame2023}, consensus-based methods leverage the graph Laplacian for formation coordination \cite{b_ren2005,b_olfati2004}, control barrier functions provide safety guarantees \cite{b_ames2017}, cooperative tracking with weighted topology has been applied to pursuit games \cite{b_zhou2024}, and RL-based formation-surrounding control has been explored for quadrotor PE scenarios \cite{b_formrl2024}. Nash strategies under limited observations have also been studied for PE differential games \cite{b_graphswap}.

Despite this rich body of work, simultaneously achieving efficient individual optimal control, flexible target assignment, and effective formation coordination within a unified framework for nonlinear systems with actuator saturation remains an open challenge. Existing approaches tend to address one or two of these aspects, but integrating all three in a coherent architecture---particularly one that admits a rigorous value-function interpretation---has not been fully explored.

This paper proposes a hierarchical cooperative control framework built around the concept of an \emph{augmented value function}. The framework comprises three layers:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Individual control layer}: The V-SNAC (Value function-based Single Network Adaptive Critic) architecture derives $\tanh$-saturated control policies directly from the value-function gradient, requiring only one critic network per pursuer and reducing the total network count from $2(N_p + N_e)$ to $N_p$. The evader's worst-case control is derived in closed form from the same value function. Weights are updated via off-policy gradient descent with a decaying learning rate for stable convergence.
\item \textbf{Target assignment layer}: A graph-theoretic pairwise swap algorithm dynamically reassigns pursuer--evader pairings based on real-time states, ensuring monotonic improvement in team cohesion.
\item \textbf{Communication coordination layer}: An intra-group communication graph connects pursuers targeting the same evader. An analytical coordination potential $\Phi_j$ is combined with the learned value function to form an augmented value function $V_j^{\mathrm{aug}}$. The pursuer control is derived from the gradient of $V_j^{\mathrm{aug}}$ inside the $\tanh$ saturation, encouraging spatial diversity while respecting actuator bounds. The coordination signal degrades gracefully under communication loss, and we establish the connection to potential-based reward shaping \cite{b_ng1999}.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~II introduces the system model, cost functional, V-SNAC control framework including the evader's worst-case strategy, and the dynamic target assignment mechanism. Section~III presents the communication-augmented control theory, including the augmented value function, the resulting control laws for both pursuers and evader, and theoretical properties. Section~IV provides experimental results across multiple scenarios. Section~V draws conclusions.

\section{Preliminaries}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Agent Dynamics}

Consider a PE game with $N_p$ pursuers and $N_e$ evaders ($N_p \geq N_e$). Each agent obeys the control-affine nonlinear dynamics:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dynamics}
\dot{x}_j = f(x_j) + g(x_j)\,u_j, \quad \|u_j\| \leq \bar{u}
\end{equation}
with state $x_j = [p_j^T, v_j^T]^T \in \mathbb{R}^n$ comprising position and velocity, and saturated control input $u_j \in \mathbb{R}^m$. This work employs a six-DOF aircraft model whose drift $f(x)$ includes aerodynamic forces (thrust, drag, lift, pitching moment) and whose input matrix satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:g_struct}
g(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 0_{3\times 3} \\ G_v(x) \end{bmatrix}, \quad G_v \approx I_3
\end{equation}
so that control inputs act as accelerations in the velocity channels.

\subsection{Cost Functional and HJI Equation}

Pursuer $j$ is assigned to evader $\sigma(j)$. The tracking error is defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:error}
\tilde{x}_j = x_j^p - x_{\sigma(j)}^e + r_{j,\sigma(j)}
\end{equation}
where $r_{j,\sigma(j)}$ encodes a desired displacement for surrounding formation. The zero-sum cost functional for the pursuer--evader pair is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cost}
J_j = \int_0^\infty \!\Big[\tilde{x}_j^T Q\,\tilde{x}_j + U(u_j^p) - W(u_e)\Big]\,dt
\end{equation}
with non-quadratic saturation-handling terms that arise from the Legendre transform under bounded controls:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nq}
U(u) = \sum_{l=1}^m 2\bar{u}\,R_{1,l}\int_0^{u_l/\bar{u}} \bar{u}\,\mathrm{arctanh}(\tau)\,d\tau
\end{equation}
and $W(u_e)$ defined analogously with penalty matrix $R_2$ and bound $\bar{u}_e$.

The saddle-point value function $V_j^*(\tilde{x}_j)$ satisfies the HJI equation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hji}
\begin{split}
0 ={} & \tilde{x}_j^T Q\,\tilde{x}_j + (\nabla V_j^*)^T f_j \\
& + \min_{u_j^p}\max_{u_e} \Big[U(u_j^p) - W(u_e) \\
& \qquad + (\nabla V_j^*)^T (g_j u_j^p - g_e u_e)\Big]
\end{split}
\end{equation}

\subsection{V-SNAC Optimal Control}

The stationarity conditions of the Hamiltonian with respect to the pursuer and evader controls yield the Nash equilibrium strategies in closed form.

\textit{Pursuer optimal control.} Setting $\partial H/\partial u_j^p = 0$ and exploiting the structure of the non-quadratic penalty $U$ gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ctrl_pursuer}
u_j^{p*} = -\bar{u}_p\,\tanh\!\bigg(\frac{1}{2\bar{u}_p}\,R_1^{-1}\,g_j^T\,\nabla V_j^*\bigg)
\end{equation}
The $\tanh$ nonlinearity naturally enforces $\|u_j^{p*}\| \leq \bar{u}_p$.

\textit{Evader worst-case control.} Analogously, setting $\partial H/\partial u_e = 0$ gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ctrl_evader}
u_e^{*} = -\bar{u}_e\,\tanh\!\bigg(\frac{1}{2\bar{u}_e}\,R_2^{-1}\,g_e^T\,\nabla V_j^*\bigg)
\end{equation}
The evader maximizes the cost, but the $\tanh$ form emerges from the same variational structure. Since the evader's control enters $J_j$ with a negative sign, the resulting policy drives the evader away from the pursuer.

In the V-SNAC framework \cite{b_xu2024}, $V_j$ is approximated as a linear-in-parameter function:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vsnac}
\hat{V}_j(\tilde{x}_j) = \hat{W}_j^T\,\psi(\tilde{x}_j)
\end{equation}
with six polynomial basis functions:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:basis}
\psi(\tilde{x}) = g_\psi\!\cdot\!\big[p_1 v_1,\; p_2 v_2,\; p_3 v_3,\; \tfrac{1}{2}v_1^2,\; \tfrac{1}{2}v_2^2,\; \tfrac{1}{2}v_3^2\big]^T
\end{equation}
where $g_\psi$ is a scaling constant and $(p_i, v_i)$ denote the position and velocity components of $\tilde{x}$. This basis captures the essential coupling between position error and velocity that drives optimal interception. Compared to actor-critic structures requiring $2(N_p + N_e)$ networks, V-SNAC needs only $N_p$ critic networks. For example, the 3v3 configuration reduces from 12 to 3 networks (75\%), and the 6v3 from 18 to 6 (67\%).

Weights are updated via off-policy least-squares gradient descent:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weight_update}
\hat{W}_{j,s+1} = \hat{W}_{j,s} - \alpha_s\,e_{j,s}\,K_{j,s}^T, \quad \alpha_s = \alpha_0\,\lambda^s
\end{equation}
where $e_{j,s}$ is the temporal-difference error, $K_{j,s}$ is a regression direction, and the exponentially decaying learning rate $\alpha_s$ with $\lambda \in (0,1)$ produces a natural ``fast descent $\to$ plateau'' convergence profile. The weights converge to a neighborhood of the true value-function parameters with uniformly ultimately bounded (UUB) error \cite{b_lewis1998}.

\subsection{Dynamic Target Assignment}

In multi-evader scenarios, pursuer--evader pairings are dynamically adjusted through a pairwise swap algorithm \cite{b_graphswap,b_xu2024}. For any pursuer pair $(j, j')$ currently assigned to evaders $(i, i')$, the algorithm evaluates whether swapping the assignments reduces the weighted distance sum by more than a threshold $\varrho > 0$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:swap}
d(j,i) + d(j',i') - d(j,i') - d(j',i) > \varrho
\end{equation}
If so, the swap is executed. This greedy mechanism guarantees monotonic reduction of the team state error $E_{\mathrm{team}}$ and converges to a locally optimal assignment. The threshold $\varrho$ prevents oscillatory behavior near equilibria.

\section{Communication-Augmented Control}
% =====================================================================

The individual control and target assignment layers address tracking optimality and assignment efficiency, respectively, but do not account for the spatial relationships among pursuers assigned to the same evader. When multiple pursuers independently optimize similar tracking objectives, they may converge to nearly identical approach trajectories---reducing encirclement efficiency and increasing the risk of inter-vehicle proximity violations in physical deployments \cite{b_icao_separation,b_collision_uav}. The communication coordination layer resolves this through a principled augmentation of the value function.

\subsection{Intra-Group Communication Graph}

A communication graph $\mathcal{G}_p = (\mathcal{V}_p, \mathcal{E}_p)$ connects pursuers targeting the same evader:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:adj}
a_{jk} = \begin{cases} 1, & \sigma(j) = \sigma(k),\; j \neq k \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
with degree $d_j = \sum_k a_{jk}$. The graph topology evolves with the target assignment: when the pairwise swap algorithm reassigns a pursuer, the adjacency matrix updates accordingly. The intra-group restriction ensures that coordination acts only among agents whose approach trajectories are genuinely at risk of overlap, following the locality principle advocated in consensus-based coordination \cite{b_ren2005,b_olfati2004}.

\subsection{Coordination Potential}

For each pursuer $j$ with $d_j > 0$, we define a pairwise repulsion kernel $\varphi: \mathbb{R}_+ \to \mathbb{R}_+$ and a coordination potential:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi}
\Phi_j(p_j, \{p_k\}) = \frac{\gamma}{d_j}\sum_{k:\,a_{jk}>0} \varphi(\|p_j - p_k\|)
\end{equation}
where $\gamma > 0$ controls the coordination strength. The repulsion kernel is chosen as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernel}
\varphi(r) = \frac{1}{r + \epsilon}
\end{equation}
with $\epsilon > 0$ preventing singularity at zero separation. This kernel is monotonically decreasing, so $\Phi_j$ is large when pursuers are close and vanishes as they separate---precisely the behavior needed to discourage trajectory overlap. The degree-normalized averaging $1/d_j$ ensures that the coordination strength remains consistent regardless of group size.

\subsection{Augmented Value Function}

The key theoretical construct is the \emph{augmented value function}, which decomposes into a learned tracking component and an analytical coordination component:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vaug}
V_j^{\mathrm{aug}}(\tilde{x}_j, p_j, \{p_k\}) = \hat{W}_j^T\,\psi(\tilde{x}_j) + \Phi_j(p_j, \{p_k\})
\end{equation}
The first term $\hat{W}_j^T\psi(\tilde{x}_j)$ is learned by V-SNAC and encodes the individual pursuit-evasion optimality. The second term $\Phi_j$ is analytical and encodes the team coordination objective. This decomposition has several important properties.

The gradient of $V_j^{\mathrm{aug}}$ with respect to the state decomposes correspondingly:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grad_aug}
\nabla V_j^{\mathrm{aug}} = (\nabla \psi)^T \hat{W}_j + \nabla \Phi_j
\end{equation}
where $\nabla \Phi_j$ is computed from the kernel gradient:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grad_phi}
\nabla_{p_j} \Phi_j = -\frac{\gamma}{d_j}\sum_{k:\,a_{jk}>0} \frac{p_j - p_k}{\|p_j - p_k\|(\|p_j - p_k\| + \epsilon)^2}
\end{equation}
This gradient is embedded in the velocity subspace through the input matrix structure (\ref{eq:g_struct}).

\subsection{Augmented Control Laws}

\textit{Pursuer optimal control.} Applying the stationarity condition $\partial H^{\mathrm{aug}}/\partial u_j^p = 0$ to the augmented Hamiltonian yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ctrl_aug}
u_j^{p*} = -\bar{u}_p\,\tanh\!\bigg(\frac{1}{2\bar{u}_p}\,R_1^{-1}\,g_j^T\,\nabla V_j^{\mathrm{aug}}\bigg)
\end{equation}
Since $\Phi_j$ does not depend on $u_j^p$, the variational structure is identical to the standard HJI case: $\partial H^{\mathrm{aug}}/\partial u_j^p = 0$ produces the same $\tanh$ form, but evaluated at the augmented gradient $\nabla V_j^{\mathrm{aug}}$ instead of $\nabla V_j^*$. Both the learned tracking gradient and the analytical coordination gradient are summed \emph{inside} the $\tanh$, so the actuator saturation bound $\|u_j^{p*}\| \leq \bar{u}_p$ is automatically maintained.

When $\gamma = 0$ or $d_j = 0$ (solo pursuer), $\Phi_j = 0$ and (\ref{eq:ctrl_aug}) reduces to the baseline (\ref{eq:ctrl_pursuer}).

\textit{Evader worst-case control.} The coordination potential $\Phi_j$ depends only on pursuer positions and does not involve any evader state. Consequently, the evader's control problem is unaffected by the coordination layer, and the worst-case evader strategy remains:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ctrl_evader_aug}
u_e^{*} = -\bar{u}_e\,\tanh\!\bigg(\frac{1}{2\bar{u}_e}\,R_2^{-1}\,g_e^T\,\nabla V_j^*\bigg)
\end{equation}
where $\nabla V_j^*$ is the tracking value gradient only. This is physically intuitive: the evader has no knowledge of or interest in how the pursuers coordinate among themselves; it simply plays the worst-case strategy against each individual pursuit.

\subsection{Theoretical Properties}

\textit{Augmented HJI equation.} The augmented value function $V_j^{\mathrm{aug}}$ satisfies an augmented HJI equation in which the stage cost includes a coordination running cost $\ell_\Phi$ in addition to the standard tracking cost:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aug_hji}
\begin{split}
0 ={} & \tilde{x}_j^T Q\,\tilde{x}_j + \ell_\Phi + U(u_j^{p*}) - W(u_e^*) \\
& + (\nabla V_j^{\mathrm{aug}})^T [f_j + g_j u_j^{p*} - g_e u_e^*]
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $\ell_\Phi = \dot{\Phi}_j - (\nabla \Phi_j)^T g_j u_j^{p*}$ absorbs the time derivative of the potential along the augmented trajectory. This establishes that the augmented control is optimal with respect to a modified cost that penalizes both tracking error and inter-pursuer proximity.

\textit{Connection to potential-based reward shaping.} The decomposition (\ref{eq:vaug}) is an instance of potential-based reward shaping in the sense of Ng et al.\ \cite{b_ng1999}: the coordination potential $\Phi_j$ depends only on the state (pursuer positions), not on actions. A classical result in reward shaping theory states that adding a state-dependent potential to the value function preserves the optimal policy structure while redirecting exploration \cite{b_ng1999}. In our setting, $\Phi_j$ redirects the pursuit trajectories toward spatial diversity without altering the asymptotic tracking guarantee.

\textit{Independence of learning and coordination.} The V-SNAC weight update depends only on the tracking Bellman residual and is independent of $\Phi$. Since $\Phi$ is analytical (not learned), it can be computed in real time from teammate positions at any stage---during online learning or deployment---without affecting the weight convergence. This preserves the UUB guarantee \cite{b_lewis1998} and allows runtime tuning of $\gamma$ without retraining.

\textit{Communication loss robustness.} Edge removal in $\mathcal{G}_p$ reduces $d_j$ and the coordination gradient proportionally. Complete communication loss sets $\nabla \Phi_j = 0$, recovering the V-SNAC baseline. For partial dropout with probability $p_d$, the Lipschitz property of $\tanh$ (Lipschitz constant 1) yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dropout}
\begin{split}
\|u_j^{\text{full}} - u_j^{\text{drop}}\| \leq {} & \frac{1}{2\bar{u}_p}\|R_1^{-1}\|\,\|G_v\|_\infty \\
& \cdot \|\nabla\Phi_j^{\text{full}} - \nabla\Phi_j^{\text{drop}}\|
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Since $\nabla \Phi_j$ is bounded by $\gamma/\epsilon^2$, the control perturbation is bounded and proportional to $\gamma$, ensuring graceful degradation.

\section{Experiment and Result Analysis}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

Experiments use a six-DOF nonlinear aircraft model with state $x = [p_x, p_y, h, v_x, v_y, v_h]^T$. Pursuer saturation is $\bar{u}_p = 25$ and evader saturation is $\bar{u}_e = 15$, reflecting the pursuers' speed advantage. Each V-SNAC critic uses the six polynomial basis functions in (\ref{eq:basis}), trained for 50--56 policy iterations with decaying learning rate $\alpha_s = 0.05 \times 0.85^s$. The coordination strength is $\gamma = 0.3$ and the singularity guard is $\epsilon = 0.1$.

Five team configurations are tested: 3v1, 3v3, 5v3, 6v3, and 8v4, spanning pursuer-to-evader ratios from 1 to 3. The same trained weights are evaluated under three communication modes: full communication ($\gamma = 0.3$), no communication ($\gamma = 0$), and dropout communication ($\gamma = 0.3$, per-edge dropout probability $p_d = 0.15$). Under the dropout mode, each communication link in $\mathcal{G}_p$ is independently removed with probability $p_d$ at each time step; both directions of an edge are dropped together to maintain adjacency symmetry. The penalty matrices are $Q = I_6$, $R_1 = 0.1 I_3$, and $R_2 = 0.1 I_3$.

The primary metric for coordination effectiveness is the minimum inter-pursuer distance $d_{\min} = \min_{j < k}\|p_j - p_k\|$, which quantifies spatial separation during the pursuit process. Capture time is reported as a secondary metric to verify that the coordination layer does not critically degrade tracking performance.

\subsection{Coordination Effectiveness: Controlled Scenario}

To isolate the effect of the coordination potential, we construct a controlled scenario: two pursuers start from nearby positions with identical displacement targets and initial velocities. Without coordination, their tracking errors and controls are identical at every time step, producing completely overlapping trajectories. This scenario directly demonstrates why independent optimal control is insufficient when multiple pursuers share a target.

Table~\ref{tab:results} reports capture time and minimum inter-pursuer distance $d_{\min}$ across five configurations. The $d_{\min}$ column reveals the core effect of the coordination potential: \emph{communication consistently increases inter-pursuer spatial separation in every scenario with intra-group edges}. In the 3v1 case, $d_{\min}$ more than doubles from 136\,m to 282\,m; in the 6v3 case it increases by 22\% (101\,m $\to$ 123\,m); and the 5v3 and 8v4 cases show 6--10\% improvements.

This spatial improvement entails an expected trade-off in capture time. Because the $\tanh$-saturated controller has finite control authority, the coordination gradient diverts part of that authority from tracking to separation. In the 3v1 and 8v4 scenarios, capture time increases by 8\,s and 1\,s respectively. In the 5v3 case, only one evader has two co-assigned pursuers, so the coordination effect on capture time is negligible. In the 6v3 case, diversified approach angles reduce mutual interference during encirclement and allow the dynamic assignment algorithm to find more efficient pairings, yielding \emph{both} better separation and faster capture.

The 3v3 scenario ($N_p/N_e = 1$) serves as a natural control group: each evader has exactly one pursuer, so $d_j = 0$ for all $j$ and the coordination mechanism is correctly inactive. All three modes produce identical results.

Under 15\% per-edge dropout, $d_{\min}$ remains close to the full-communication values, and capture times deviate by at most 1.2\,s. This is consistent with the bounded perturbation analysis in (\ref{eq:dropout}).

We present detailed analysis for three representative scenarios: 3v1 ($N_p/N_e = 3$, highest coordination demand), 6v3 ($N_p/N_e = 2$, multi-evader with dynamic reassignment), and 3v3 ($N_p/N_e = 1$, control group).

During the initial phase ($t < 20$\,s), the pursuer controls saturate at $\pm\bar{u}_p = \pm 25$, reflecting the aggressive maneuvers needed to close the large initial gap. As the tracking errors decrease, the controls transition to the linear regime of the $\tanh$ function. The evader's control saturates at $\pm\bar{u}_e = \pm 15$, confirming that the worst-case evasion strategy is being executed at the actuator limits---the evader exploits its full control authority to escape. The smooth transition from saturation to linear operation demonstrates the effectiveness of the non-quadratic cost formulation in handling actuator bounds.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

The 3v3 scenario with $N_p/N_e = 1$ provides an important validation: since each evader has exactly one pursuer, the intra-group graph has no edges and $d_j = 0$ for all pursuers. \section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================

This paper presented a hierarchical cooperative control framework for multi-pursuer pursuit--evasion games under nonlinear dynamics with actuator saturation. The central theoretical contribution is the augmented value function, which decomposes into a learned tracking component (via V-SNAC) and an analytical coordination potential, with both gradients combined inside the $\tanh$ saturation to yield the pursuer control. The evader's worst-case control is derived from the tracking value alone, since the coordination potential involves only pursuer states. The augmented formulation satisfies an augmented HJI equation and connects to potential-based reward shaping theory.

The three-layer framework---individual V-SNAC control, dynamic target assignment, and communication-based coordination---addresses individual optimality, team assignment, and intra-group spatial diversity respectively. Experiments on a nonlinear six-DOF aircraft model across five team configurations (3v1 through 8v4) demonstrated: (i)~consistent improvement of inter-pursuer spatial separation through the coordination potential, with $d_{\min}$ increasing by 7--108\% across all scenarios with intra-group edges; (ii)~an expected and bounded trade-off in capture time, where the finite control authority shared between tracking and coordination results in at most 8\,s additional capture time; (iii)~graceful degradation under 15\% per-edge communication dropout; and (iv)~correct deactivation when no coordination is needed. Future work will investigate adaptive coordination strength scheduling based on real-time proximity, extension to heterogeneous agent dynamics, and explicit communication delay modeling.
